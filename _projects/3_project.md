## Overview

I participated in the NSF I-Corps program as the Entrepreneurial Lead for a porous silicon (PSi) biosensor platform developed at Vanderbilt University. The project explored how a quantitative, low-cost biosensing technology could be translated from an academic prototype into a commercially viable healthcare product.

Over the course of the program, our team conducted 100+ customer discovery interviews across patients, pet owners, physicians, veterinarians, diagnostic companies, payers, startup founders, investors, and MedTech commercialization leaders.

The project began with chronic kidney disease (CKD) monitoring in humans and evolved toward feline CKD as customer discovery revealed a more concentrated monitoring burden and clearer caregiver pain points. Ultimately, the program also uncovered a critical product–market fit gap between the current whole-blood sensing platform and customers' preference for lower-burden, minimally invasive monitoring.

---

## Motivation

Our initial hypothesis was that patients with chronic kidney disease could benefit from more frequent quantitative monitoring between conventional laboratory visits.

The underlying research combined porous silicon optical biosensors with paper-based microfluidics to improve biomolecular transport and quantitative sensing while reducing the infrastructure required for testing.

However, technical performance alone could not answer several critical commercialization questions:

- Who experiences the most urgent monitoring problem?
- Who would actually use and pay for the technology?
- What information is clinically actionable?
- How would home-generated data fit into existing clinical workflows?
- What capabilities should an early-stage team build internally versus obtain through partners?

NSF I-Corps provided a structured framework to test these assumptions directly with prospective customers and stakeholders rather than relying on technology-driven assumptions.

## Approach

### Human CKD Customer Discovery

We initially investigated longitudinal monitoring for human CKD.

Interviews with patients, nephrologists, nurses, and remote-patient-monitoring stakeholders revealed that CKD management needs varied substantially based on disease severity, geography, healthcare access, and available clinical resources.

While the overall market was large, we struggled to identify a sufficiently narrow customer segment experiencing the same urgent problem.

`Key learning: A large total addressable market does not necessarily provide a clear beachhead market.`

---

### Pivot to Feline CKD

Customer discovery led us to explore feline CKD, where repeated testing, difficult blood collection, clinic-related stress, and caregiver burden were recurring themes.

We interviewed CKD cat owners, veterinarians, veterinary specialists, insurers, diagnostic companies, and home-monitoring companies.

The strongest emerging segment was highly engaged caregivers managing cats requiring repeated monitoring, particularly in more advanced CKD.

`Key learning: Customers were not simply seeking more measurements. They wanted low-burden longitudinal information that could help determine when veterinary care should change.`

---

### Clinical Workflow Validation

Veterinarian interviews further refined the concept.

Clinicians consistently emphasized that home-generated data would only be useful if it were:

- reliable
- clinically interpretable
- actionable
- easy to integrate into veterinary workflow
- unlikely to create excessive review burden

Feline CKD specialists also identified a significant limitation in our original concept: owner-collected whole blood is difficult, stressful, and potentially unreliable in cats.

This created an important product–market fit gap.

Customer need: low-burden longitudinal monitoring
Current platform: quantitative sensing using whole blood

This shifted our technical questions toward urine, saliva, hydration, blood pressure, and other minimally invasive monitoring strategies.

---

### Commercialization and Partnership Discovery

At The MedTech Conference and through additional interviews, I spoke with leaders from diagnostics, medical devices, consulting, market access, strategic partnerships, and startup companies.

Recurring commercialization requirements included:

Clinical utility
- Does the measurement actually change diagnosis, treatment, or follow-up?

Workflow fit
- Can the technology integrate into existing care without adding excessive burden?

Partnership readiness
- Does the technology have publications, IP, a functional prototype, relevant biological-sample validation, and strategic fit?

Scalability
- Can manufacturing, quality control, distribution, and service delivery scale economically?

Company readiness
- Does the team have sufficient funding, runway, and external validation to survive the commercialization process?

One important strategic insight was that established companies evaluate technologies based on the real-world problem they solve, not simply technical novelty.

## Accomplishments & Outcomes

### 100+ Customer Discovery Interviews

Conducted customer discovery across multiple stakeholder groups, including:

- Human CKD patients and caregivers
- Nephrologists and nurses
- Remote-patient-monitoring professionals
- Feline CKD caregivers
- Veterinarians and veterinary specialists
- Pet-insurance stakeholders
- Veterinary diagnostic and monitoring companies
- MedTech executives and startup founders
- Market-access and reimbursement experts
- Manufacturing and scale-up professionals
- Strategic partnership and acquisition leaders


### Identified and Tested Multiple Business Model Pivots

Initial hypothesis

- Human CKD patients → frequent quantitative home monitoring

Customer-discovery pivot

- Feline CKD caregivers → low-burden longitudinal monitoring

Refined value proposition

- Actionable longitudinal monitoring that helps caregivers and veterinarians identify meaningful changes and determine when care should change.

### Identified a Critical Product–Market Fit Gap

Customer discovery validated a meaningful monitoring problem but also demonstrated that the current whole-blood technology does not align well with the preferred feline home-monitoring workflow.

This finding directly changed our technical-development priorities.

The most clinically informative sample is not necessarily the most usable sample.

Rather than forcing the existing technology into the market, we chose to investigate alternative sample modalities and use cases.

### Developed a Partnering Strategy

Customer discovery helped distinguish which capabilities should remain internal versus be obtained through external partners.

- Core capabilities to retain
- PSi sensing technology
- Assay development
- Core IP
- Analytical validation
- Customer and workflow knowledge
- Potential partnership capabilities
- Veterinary clinical validation
- Manufacturing and scale-up
- Regulatory and reimbursement strategy
- Distribution
- Data/service infrastructure
- Strategic commercialization

### Refined Commercialization Readiness Criteria

Interviews with strategic partners and MedTech leaders indicated that external companies evaluate university technologies based on a combination of:

Scientific evidence → IP → Clinical utility → Prototype → Real biological samples → Workflow fit → Strategic fit → Scalability

This framework now guides the next stage of technology development.

### Final Path Forward
The program confirmed that a real customer need exists, but did not yet validate product–market fit for the current whole-blood platform.

Based on the customer-discovery evidence, our team selected:

- Path Forward 3 — Additional Customer Discovery and Technical Research

The next phase focuses on:

- Evaluating lower-burden and non-invasive sample modalities
- Identifying the most clinically actionable measurements
- Validating the technology using relevant biological samples
- Further defining veterinary workflow integration
- Assessing clinic-based point-of-care applications as an alternative to owner-operated testing
- Continuing strategic partnership discussions

The central lesson from the project was:
A technically strong sensor is only one component of a successful product. Commercial value emerges when the technology solves the right problem, fits the customer workflow, changes a meaningful decision, and can be delivered through a sustainable business model.
